# Copywriting & Argument Structure

A page's visual impact fails if the underlying words are generic or templated. Craft the copy deck as plain text before writing any markup or styling.

---

## 1. The Argument Deck: Claim & Proof

Before designing any layout, outline the entire page as a sequence of **claims** backed by **proof**, one line per section:

```text
Section 1 (Hero): Claim: [Core value proposition] — Proof: [Immediate visual or metric proof]
Section 2: Claim: [Why this approach works] — Proof: [Concrete architectural / structural detail]
Section 3: Claim: [Tangible results] — Proof: [Real data, verified benchmark, or live artifact]
```

- **Section Selection**: The claims dictate which sections exist and their chronological order.
- **Eliminate Fluff**: If a section does not carry a distinct claim and proof, delete it. This prevents falling into the robotic AI sequence of *Hero → Logos → Three Feature Cards → Fake Testimonial → Pricing Table → Generic CTA*.

---

## 2. The Two-Line Headline Rule & Arithmetic

Every headline (`h1` through `h6`) must fit into **two lines or fewer at every viewport width** (desktop, tablet, mobile).

- **Character Arithmetic**: Aim for **30 to 36 characters** (approximately 4 to 6 words) for primary headlines.
  * In the study of 247 award-winning gallery sites, the median hero headline was **5 words and 32 characters**.
  * A headline that wraps into three or four lines loses punch, dominates too much vertical space, and fails inspection.
- **Trimming Priority**:
  1. Cut redundant filler words first.
  2. Widen the container / measure second.
  3. Reduce font size only as a final resort.

---

## 3. First-Screen Hero Structure

The opening screen must decisively answer the visitor's primary questions within five seconds:
1. **The Title (`h1`)**: Crisp, confident statement of what this is or does (in two lines or fewer).
2. **One Supporting Sentence**: Placed directly beneath the headline (32–48px gap), fully visible on initial render (never hidden behind delayed entrance animations). If the headline is metaphorical, this sentence states plainly and directly what the thing is.
3. **One Primary Action**: A clear button named specifically for what it performs (e.g. "Explore Challenges", "Download Binary", "Book Session"), rather than a vague "Get Started" or "Learn More".

---

## 4. Full Content Standards vs. Thin Placeholders

- **Complete Information**: Provide full, rich content rather than brief summaries. If showing a curriculum, list all modules; if showing pricing, detail exactly what is included. Thin sections are the most common reason clean designs feel hollow.
- **Plausible Specifics vs. Invented Proof**:
  * When details are unspecified in a brief, write realistic sample domain data (e.g., specific challenge names, vulnerability descriptions, system specs).
  * **Never invent social proof**: Absolutely no fabricated client logos, fake customer testimonials, fictitious press quotes, or made-up corporate statistics.
